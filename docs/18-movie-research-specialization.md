# 18 — Movie Research Specialization

## Goal

The investigator should feel like a top documentary researcher, film scholar, script analyst, archivist, and production researcher working as one coordinated system. It must not behave like a generic web-search agent with movie vocabulary.

## Research axes

Every case may activate one or more axes.

### 1. Film text

Research the movie as an authored audiovisual object:

- scene order and dramatic structure;
- dialogue and screenplay language;
- framing, blocking, lensing, camera movement, and composition;
- editing rhythm, ellipsis, montage, continuity, and parallel cutting;
- color, light, production design, costume, props, and recurring visual motifs;
- performance choices;
- score, sound design, silence, and source music;
- opening, ending, credits, and title design;
- explicit and implied point of view.

The system may analyze user-provided frames or permitted clips. It must not pretend to have watched a full copyrighted film when no lawful film-text input is available.

### 2. Script and development

- screenplay drafts;
- treatments and outlines;
- deleted or revised scenes;
- studio notes where legitimately available;
- storyboards and concept art;
- casting changes;
- test screenings;
- censorship or ratings changes;
- production delays and rewrites.

### 3. Authorship and collaboration

Research the relevant creative voices separately:

- director;
- screenwriter;
- source author;
- cinematographer;
- editor;
- composer;
- production designer;
- costume designer;
- visual-effects supervisor;
- actors;
- producers and studio.

Do not flatten a film into “the director intended.” Film meaning may emerge from collaboration, accident, constraint, or reception.

### 4. Versions and cuts

Track the exact version under discussion:

- theatrical, director’s, extended, festival, broadcast, airline, international, censored, restored, or alternate cut;
- runtime;
- territory;
- language and subtitle track;
- release medium;
- scene additions, removals, and reordered material.

A theory based on one cut must not silently transfer to another.

### 5. Adaptation and source material

- novel, article, biography, myth, play, game, historical record, prior film, or folklore source;
- material retained, compressed, invented, inverted, or removed;
- adaptation choices and their thematic effects;
- translation and edition differences.

### 6. Real-world context

- history and politics;
- military or legal record;
- science and technology;
- psychology and medicine;
- religion and mythology;
- geography and culture;
- institutions and economics.

For “based on a true story” work, distinguish cinematic causality from the historical record.

### 7. Reception and interpretation

- contemporary reviews;
- later criticism;
- scholarly analysis;
- festival and awards context;
- cultural impact;
- interviews after release;
- fan and community theories.

Community interpretation is useful as a lead and reception record, not automatically as evidence of authorial intention.

### 8. Influence and intertext

- films, books, paintings, music, architecture, myths, and events cited or plausibly referenced;
- direct acknowledgement versus inferred similarity;
- recurring themes across a filmmaker’s work;
- production lineage and genre conventions.

## Movie-source hierarchy

Use the hierarchy contextually rather than mechanically.

1. The exact film version and user-provided scene evidence.
2. Screenplays, production documents, commentary, and first-party interviews.
3. Contemporaneous reporting and reputable trade coverage.
4. Books, archives, biographies, and scholarly work.
5. High-quality criticism and specialist analysis.
6. Community observations and fan theories.
7. Unsourced reposts, AI-generated pages, quote aggregators, and derivative summaries.

A first-party interview can still be self-serving, retrospective, inconsistent, or promotional. Record the date and context.

## Required source diversity

For a major theory branch, attempt to obtain evidence from at least three distinct classes when available:

- film-text evidence;
- production or creator evidence;
- independent contextual or critical evidence.

Do not manufacture diversity when the topic only has one credible source class.

## Specialist research playbooks

### Symbolism theory

1. Locate every occurrence of the object, color, phrase, sound, or composition.
2. Track changes in context and character relationship.
3. Check screenplay and production-design sources.
4. Search creator commentary without treating intent as the only valid reading.
5. Find competing interpretations.
6. Test whether the pattern is more explanatory than coincidence.

### Ending interpretation

1. Establish the exact cut.
2. Reconstruct the final sequence shot by shot from permitted material or verified descriptions.
3. Separate literal events from subjective or symbolic readings.
4. Inspect script drafts and creator statements.
5. compare alternative explanations against all earlier setup.
6. identify what the film intentionally leaves undecidable.

### True-story investigation

1. Build a film timeline and historical timeline separately.
2. Identify composites, omissions, chronology compression, and invented dialogue.
3. prioritize official records, first-hand accounts, and reputable histories.
4. preserve contested testimony.
5. explain why the adaptation may have changed events.

### Influence claim

1. Search explicit acknowledgements first.
2. establish chronology and access plausibility.
3. compare form, not just broad theme.
4. distinguish influence, homage, genre convention, and coincidence.
5. avoid claiming causation from resemblance alone.

## Domain vocabulary the agent must understand

The agent should correctly reason about mise-en-scène, diegetic and non-diegetic sound, match cut, montage, blocking, eyeline, focal length, aspect ratio, production design, motif, unreliable narration, dramatic irony, adaptation compression, composite characters, coverage, continuity, color pipeline, practical and digital effects, score leitmotif, theatrical versus director’s cut, paratext, reception history, and auteur theory—without forcing jargon into the user experience.
